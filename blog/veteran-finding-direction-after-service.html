From Two War Zones to Finding Direction

By Jason Gentrup · July 30, 2026 · 5 min read

Jason Gentrup grew up in Belle Fourche, South Dakota, close to the literal geographic center of the United States. It's a fitting starting point for a life that would eventually travel to six continents and more than thirty countries in search of a different kind of center: the internal one. But that search didn't begin with curiosity. It began with the Marine Corps, two deployments, and everything that came home with him afterward.

Two Deployments, and What Came Home With Him

Jason served in the United States Marine Corps, completing deployments to both Iraq and Afghanistan. War does not leave people the way it found them, and Jason's deployments were no exception. Among the weight he carried home was the loss of his lieutenant, the kind of loss that reorganizes how a person understands risk, loyalty, and what it means to keep going when someone else didn't get to.

Coming home is often described as the easy part. It rarely is. The skills and reflexes that keep a person alive in a war zone don't simply switch off at the airport. They come home too, and they have to be renegotiated against an entirely different kind of life.

The Low Point

For Jason, that renegotiation included PTSD, single parenthood, and a period he has described as hitting rock bottom in Florida. It's an honest, unglamorous part of the story, and it belongs in it: the version of this journey that skips the low point isn't the real one. Raising a daughter alone while carrying the invisible weight of two deployments and an unprocessed loss is not a background detail. It was the actual terrain he was standing on when everything started to change.

The Turn: Touch, Connection, Participation

The turn didn't come from a single dramatic moment. It came, in large part, through massage school and the practice of bodywork, an unlikely path back into a body that had spent years being managed rather than inhabited. Bodywork asks for presence. It asks a person to actually be in the room, in their hands, in someone else's pain, rather than observing life from a protective distance. For someone rebuilding a relationship with his own nervous system, that kind of grounded, physical, relational work became a way back in.

It also put him in direct, ongoing contact with other people's stories, told through the body as much as through words. Years of that kind of listening leave a mark. They teach you to notice patterns: what holds tension, what release looks like, how much of what people carry has nowhere else to go.

From Lived Experience to a Systems View of Meaning

What followed was a long season of deliberately widening the lens: travel across six continents and more than thirty countries, and academic study in Political Science, Sociology, and Urban and Regional Planning. Each of these, on its own, might look like an unrelated chapter. Together, they became the raw material for a systems-level way of thinking about how meaning forms, how it breaks down, and how it can be rebuilt, not just in one person's recovery, but in the design of communities, institutions, and everyday life.

The Human Compass is the place where all of it converges: the deployments, the loss, the low point, the bodywork, the travel, the years spent studying how systems shape the people inside them. It isn't a book written from a distance. It's a book written from inside the exact fragmentation it describes, by someone who found his way back to coherence one honest step at a time.
